Ontario Tiny Home Rules

How to live in a tiny home legally in Ontario — and the traps that make an expensive build unusable as a home.

The legal checklist

Tiny homes are legal in Ontario when all of these hold:

  1. At least 188 sq ft (17.47 m²) for a year-round dwelling.
  2. Rooms: a living/sleeping area, a bathroom with a shower or bath, and a cooking area with running water.
  3. Ontario Building Code compliance: sleeping lofts at least 6'10" high and reached by a staircase, not a ladder; fire safety and insulation standards met. The Code addresses tiny homes under 37 m² in Section 1.11.
  4. A municipal building permit. The municipality reviews the plans for Code compliance and local by-laws, and inspects during construction. Factory-built homes from a CSA-certified facility generally skip additional inspections; RV certifications do not count.
  5. Servicing: in urban and suburban areas, connection to municipal water, sewer and usually electricity is normally required.

Foundation vs. wheels — the split that decides everything

On a foundation (the open path)

Foundation-built tiny homes — garden suites, laneway houses, coach houses, detached additional units — are the broadly legal route. Bill 23 reforms allow up to three residential units on most lots. Ottawa's January 2026 zoning overhaul allows four units on every serviced residential lot, and Oakville permits garden suites as-of-right. Development charges are waived for qualifying garden suites. No rezoning is needed on most residential properties.

On wheels (the hard path)

Ontario's own guide recognizes homes on wheels as dwellings, but most municipalities treat them as mobile homes: allowed mainly in mobile home parks or campgrounds. You generally cannot place one on land — even land you own — unless your municipality explicitly allows it. A home on wheels also usually cannot have a sleeping loft under Code rules. Consider wheels only where a municipality has an explicit permitting bylaw, and verify CSA Z241 certification before buying.

The certification trap — read before you buy

Certification decides whether a build can legally be your primary residence. A build certified only under CSA Z240 RV is a recreational vehicle — it cannot be a primary residence, even at $120,000. A build certified to CSA Z240 MH can be a permanent secondary dwelling. CSA A277 covers factory certification of buildings (modular, crane-set on a permanent foundation — mortgage-eligible). Ask for the certification in writing, not in marketing words like "four-season" or "CSA-certified" without the standard named.

Fines

Building without a permit is an offence under the Building Code Act, 1992: fines up to $50,000 for a first offence and $100,000 for subsequent offences. The permit step is not optional paperwork.

Five steps, in order

  1. Check zoning first. Ask your municipality: is this lot zoned for the unit, what are the setbacks, lot coverage, frontage and servicing rules?
  2. Pick the certification path. Foundation + A277/Z240 MH for a permanent home; verify Z241 if wheels are genuinely allowed where you plan to put it.
  3. Get the building permit. Submit plans; expect inspections during construction unless factory-certified.
  4. Arrange servicing. Municipal connections in town; well, septic and hydro on rural lots (budget $80,000–$150,000 for unserviced rural servicing).
  5. Confirm year-round status. Insulation, heating and Code compliance are what separate a legal year-round home from a seasonal structure.

Where rules differ most

Toronto allows laneway and garden suites in many residential areas and up to three units on one property. Kitchener encourages tiny homes as secondary units where lot minimums are met. Hamilton, Innisfil, Brantford and London all have garden-suite or secondary-unit policies. But every one of these still runs through that municipality's own by-laws — the provincial rules set the floor, the municipality sets the details.
